Does pickle juice help muscle cramps? What the science actually says

By The DILL'D Lab · October 7, 2026 · 6 min read

On this page

Long before pickle energy drinks existed, athletes were taking shots of pickle juice on the sidelines to stop cramps. Is there anything to it? There's some interesting research, but the reason it seems to work isn't the one most people assume.

The study everyone cites

In 2010, researchers published a small study in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise on mildly dehydrated men who had cramps triggered in a lab. When they drank a small amount of pickle juice, their cramps ended noticeably faster than when they drank nothing or plain water: on average, roughly a minute and a half versus a little over two minutes.

Why it probably works (it's not the salt)

The effect showed up within about a minute and a half, which is far too fast for the sodium in the brine to be absorbed from the stomach into the blood. The leading idea is a reflex: the sharp, sour taste of vinegar triggers receptors in the mouth and throat, which may calm the misfiring nerves that keep a muscle cramped.

In other words, the "shock" of the taste might be doing the work, not electrolyte replacement.

What the research doesn't say

  • It was a small study on electrically induced cramps, not cramps during a real game.
  • It doesn't show pickle juice prevents cramps.
  • It doesn't mean salty drinks are a good way to rehydrate after hard exercise.

So what does this mean for DILL'D?

We'll be honest, because it's on our whole brand: DILL'D is a treat, not a sports drink or a medical product. It does have the sour, salty hit of real fermented dill brine and about 460mg of sodium per can, but it also has 180mg of caffeine, which is not what you want for recovery.

If you get cramps during sport, talk to a coach or a health professional. If you just love the taste of brine and want a can that wakes you up, that's exactly what we made. Start with our flavour guide.

🥒 TL;DR: a small study found pickle juice ended lab-induced cramps faster than water, probably through a taste reflex. Cool science, but DILL'D is for fun, not first aid.
